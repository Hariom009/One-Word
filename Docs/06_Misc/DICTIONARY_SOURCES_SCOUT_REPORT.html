Dictionary sources census — One Word

Brief: “for each dictionary pack we might sell, how many real sources exist, and how many of them can we legally bundle in a paid app?” (full brief in the appendix) · Mode: landscape (sources census, no named rival) · Evidence bias: research — leaned on primary licence and terms pages, because the brief asks for licence evidence; the App Store leg ran only for question E · Run: 2026-10-21 → 22 · Research, not legal advice.

Answer: we found 250 real sources across 13 packs plus the Hindi gloss, and 112 are legally usable (83 without share-alike strings). Every pack can be built, but only Medicine, Economics and Latin & Greek roots come with clean, ready-made definitions. Every other pack is “we write it ourselves” from public-domain scans and open word lists, and Feelings and the Hindi glosses are written from scratch.

TL;DR

  • Every pack can be built, but only Medicine, Economics and Latin & Greek roots have clean, ready-made modern definitions. → for us: ship those first.
  • Both Gemini claims are wrong: the Cologne Sanskrit e-text is NC-SA, and the MED is all rights reserved. → for us: build Sanskrit from 1899/1890 print scans plus the DCS word list.
  • OpenStax went CC BY-NC-SA in March 2026 and now bans LLM ingestion. → for us: pinned CC BY snapshots only, after a lawyer clears the clause for our LLM writers.
  • The public-domain books are free, but their best digital copies are not (DDSA, CDSL, Perseus, IA terms). → for us: work from page scans and log the source of every entry.
  • Feelings and Hindi glosses have no usable written source. → for us: write them ourselves, as today, with native-speaker checks.
  • Open German and Hindi sources are all share-alike or GPL. → for us: read them to check facts; never adapt their text.

Corrections

Gemini: “Cologne Sanskrit (CDSL) data is safe / public domain” — Refuted. The MW and AP90 headers read “Copyright © 2014 The Sanskrit Library and Thomas Malten”, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0. A 2026 root LICENSE says CC BY-SA 4.0; CITATION.cff says GPL-3.0. Every reading is non-commercial or share-alike. Only the 1899/1890 prints are PD.
Gemini: “the Middle English Dictionary is public domain” — Refuted. © Regents of the University of Michigan; the site “provides access to these materials for educational and research purposes”.
Brief: OpenStax textbooks as an open source — Changed in 2026. The live Psychology 2e, Sociology 3e, Economics 3e, A&P 2e, Intro to Philosophy and Business Law are CC BY-NC-SA, and every book page now bars ingestion into LLMs. Only older CC BY copies are usable.
Brief: Platts (1884) public domain — True for the print, not the e-text. DDSA's digitised Platts, Fallon, Shakespear and Steingass carry a CC BY-NC-ND 2.0 label.
Brief: US government glossaries (NIH/NCI) are public domain — Mostly. MedlinePlus' A.D.A.M. encyclopedia and drug monographs are licensed, Federal Reserve education glossaries are limited to research and informational use (read via WebFetch), and NCI wants a translation disclaimer.
Starting points: GCIDE, Open Multilingual WordNet, IndoWordNet — GCIDE is GPL v3+ (Wordnik labels it CC BY-SA — wrong). OMW has no Hindi or Urdu. IndoWordNet and Hindi WordNet show no commercial licence (UNCLEAR).

Do's & don'ts (each traced to a finding)

Do
  • Take public-domain text from page scans, not from digitised e-texts.
    why: DDSA's text is NC-ND, CDSL's is NC-SA, and Perseus bans commercial use, while the prints behind them are PD.
  • Log a compilation trail per entry: source, edition, page.
    why: OUP won Media24 v. OUP (South Africa, 2016) by documenting compilation. The Madras High Court (1954) rejects a “common source” defence without proof.
  • Pin every CC BY source by commit or date and archive its licence file.
    why: OpenStax relicensed its live editions in 2026; CC BY copies taken earlier stay CC BY.
  • Ship an in-app credits screen.
    why: NCI needs a translation disclaimer, MeSH needs its version, and every CC BY source needs a credit line.
  • Blocklist Koenig's coinages and known fake “untranslatables” (razbliuto, ilunga).
    why: Wiktionary carries Koenig's words, and linguists document fakes in the popular lists.
Don't
  • Don't put Merriam-Webster, Oxford or any all-rights-reserved dictionary into an LLM prompt, even to paraphrase it.
    why: Britannica and M-W sued OpenAI (2026) partly over reproduced definitions.
  • Don't adapt Wiktionary, OdeNet, Ding, FreeDict or GCIDE prose.
    why: Their share-alike or GPL terms would force the rewritten pack to be released free.
  • Don't use Rekhta's data or name.
    why: Its terms reserve all rights and ban scraping, and it won a trademark injunction in Delhi in April 2026.
  • Don't use shayari by Faiz, Faraz or Sahir.
    why: They are still in copyright; Ghalib and Mir are PD.
  • Don't OCR Internet Archive scans for sale until IA's terms are checked.
    why: The latest readable copy limits access to “scholarship and research purposes only”.

- We **rewrite and shorten** definitions into our own house style, and we **translate** them

  forbids scraping is still a problem.
- the date you checked

**C. Buckets.** Put every source in exactly one bucket:

| Bucket | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **USE FREELY** | Public domain, CC0, or a permissive licence allowing commercial use + modification with no strings |
| **USE WITH CREDIT** | Allows commercial use + modification; we must credit the source (e.g. CC BY) |
| **USE WITH STRINGS** | Allowed, but with a condition that costs us something: share-alike (CC BY-SA, ODbL), copyleft (GPL), etc. Spell out the cost |
| **LICENSABLE** | All rights reserved, but the owner runs a licensing programme. Give the contact/programme page and any public pricing model |
| **DON'T** | Non-commercial (NC), no-derivatives (ND), all rights reserved with no programme, or a licence you can't find |
| **UNCLEAR** | A licence exists but is ambiguous. Say what a lawyer would need to confirm |

**D. Public domain is country by country.** The app sells worldwide. For each public-domain
source, say on what basis it is public domain. The US cutoff is roughly works published before
1931 as of 2026. Flag the ones that may still be protected elsewhere (India is the author's life
+ 60 years; the EU and UK are life + 70). Also say whether the *digitised* copy we would use
carries its own terms.

**E. How competitors source content.** Which word-a-day and vocabulary apps say where their
definitions come from? Licensed Oxford / Collins / Merriam-Webster data, WordNet, Wiktionary,
their own writing? Look at app listings, credits/about pages and press. Also list any copyright
takedowns, lawsuits or App Store removals involving dictionary apps or scraped dictionary content.

**F. Feelings pack specifics.** Find open sources of real "untranslatable" emotion words: research
lexicons (check the licence of the data, not just the paper), Wiktionary categories, Wikidata.
Note how often popular "untranslatable words" lists get meanings wrong, according to native speakers.

**G. Urdu specifics.** Platts' *Dictionary of Urdu, Classical Hindi and English* (1884) and similar
old works: confirm public-domain status, and check the terms of the digitised editions
(e.g. Digital Dictionaries of South Asia). Check what Rekhta's dictionary allows. Check whether
any IndoWordNet / Hindi WordNet / Urdu WordNet licence permits commercial use.

## Evidence rules

- Every licence claim needs **the licence page link and a verbatim line (≤15 words) from it**,

- Tag every finding **OBSERVED** (you read it at the source) or **INFERRED** (your reasoning).
- Never invent a source, a licence, a count or a quote. If you can't find a licence, the bucket
  is DON'T or UNCLEAR, not a guess.
- This is research, not legal advice. Mark the sources a copyright lawyer should confirm

- Prioritise current licence pages over old mirrors and forum summaries.
